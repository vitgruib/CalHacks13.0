The Administrative
Burden of
“Defend the Spend”
By Cindy Hope and Krystal Toups

Defend the Spend
esearch Administrators have once again
risen to the occasion, navigating new and
burdensome requirements introduced by
the Administration’s “Defend the Spend”
initiative. To date, these requirements have
offered no clear added value while significantly
increasing administrative workload for recipients
and agencies.
Introduced through a series of Executive
Orders (EO) and subsequent agency implementation, Defend the Spend mandates extensive
new reporting obligations requiring justification
of each payment under covered contracts and
grants.
Executive Order 14222 — Implementing
the President’s “Department of Government
Efficiency” Cost Efficiency Initiative (February
26, 2025) — directs each agency to “record
every payment…along with a brief, written
justification for each payment submitted by the
agency employee who approved the payment.”
Creating the line-by-line justification requests
that recipients are now encountering.
Institutions have developed creative solutions
to streamline compliance with the new requirements, yet the burden remains substantial and
may grow. Executive Order 14332 — Improving
Oversight of Federal Grantmaking (August 7,
2025) — may further burden the federal
payment process with its requirement for
agencies to:
(i) prohibit recipients from directly
drawing down general grant funds for
specific projects without the affirmative
authorization of the agency; and
(ii) require grantees to provide written
explanations or support, with specificity, for requests for each drawdown.

Perhaps even more concerning is the fact that
these EOs and their implementation disregard
longstanding, effective mechanisms already in
place to ensure stewardship of federal funds.
In particular, Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200)
section 303, Internal Controls, and Subpart F,
Audit Requirements, provide a robust framework,
implementing the Single Audit Act and setting
“forth standards for obtaining consistency and
uniformity among Federal agencies for the
audit of non-Federal entities expending Federal
awards.” The federal government, of course,
has a responsibility to impose additional
requirements if needed, and the Uniform
Guidance already provides for that, but only for
the specific conditions necessary to address
the risks of a particular awardee. Under 2 CFR
200.206, the federal agency has an obligation
to conduct a risk assessment, and section 208
provides a list of risk factors to consider, along
with the specific conditions an agency may
include in an award to address any identified
risk.
EO 14222 and 14332 ignore requirements in
Uniform Guidance to use risk management and
consider internal controls. In fact, the Uniform
Guidance uses the term “risk”108 times and
“internal control” 44 times, and only imposes
“specific conditions”, like more detailed
financial reporting, on a particular entity when
that entity has risk factors, like “inadequate
financial capability to perform the Federal
award,” underscoring the critical role of
risk-based oversight (2 CFR 200.208). By
disregarding these principles, the new EOs have
overridden those practical, efficient requirements
with inefficient, risk-blind paperwork.

While institutions have managed to comply
with the unnecessary new requirements
resulting from EO 14222 and 14332 without
significantly slowing down research, the
administration’s disregard for the time-tested
system of risk assessment, internal controls,
and audits is a reason for concern. We can’t
predict what might be next or whether institutions
will have the capacity to efficiently respond to
expanding, inefficient new requirements.
Fact Sheet: Waste and Inefficiencies Due
to New Grant Requirements
To illustrate the significant waste and redundancy
resulting from DOGE’s implementation of the
Defend the Spend initiative, COGR developed
an infographic, Defend the Spend, Waste and
Inefficiencies Due to the New Grant Requirements. The analysis estimates that Defend the
Spend results in approximately 53,749 hours1
of redundant work for recipients and federal
agencies, simply to process payments to low-risk
institutions for projects and costs previously
approved by the agency (COGR, n.d.). This
redundancy offers no reduction in improper
payments, no cost savings, and no efficiency
gains.
Figure 1 demonstrates the redundancy and
inefficiencies of Defend the Spend. The new
requirement disregards the extensive cost and
process audits that recipients undergo to ensure
systems safeguard government assets and
comply with federal regulations. The additional,
ad hoc requests for supporting details, none of
which have undergone a federal rule-making
process, add no value for the taxpayer.

Lastly, the fact sheet highlights tangible
solutions the government can take to reduce
the burden, lower barriers, and engage with the
community.
Figure 2
The solutions are easy:

1

Available Information and
Processes Provide Efficiency

The government should evaluate what’s
already available, e.g., NOFOs, grant
award budgets, annual financial (SF 425)
and programmatic progress reports,
single audit reports, and individual
payment request certifications to focus
resources where they’re most needed.

2

Implement Risk-Based Oversight /
Lower Barriers

The government should follow the risk assessment
framework outlined in 2 CFR 200.206 to determine
when additional review is warranted.

3

Engage with Stakeholders

The government should conduct a cost-benefit
analysis and collaborate with recipients to identify
the most efficient way to provide any needed
additional information where genuine risks have
been identified.
